# Product positioning

Positioning is the deliberate choice of how customers should understand your product: what it is, who it is for, and why it beats what they would do otherwise. April Dunford's book Obviously Awesome (2019) breaks positioning into five components that depend on each other. Wrong positioning can make a good product look like a weaker copy of something else.

## Why it matters

Customers judge a product by comparing it with something. If you do not choose that comparison, they choose it for you, often against the market leader, where a new product loses. Positioning also tells a team which users and features to focus on.

## The idea

Dunford's five components, starting from the alternatives:

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What target customers would use if your product did not exist. Often this is not a rival product but doing nothing, a spreadsheet, or a group chat.
2. **Unique attributes.** Capabilities you have that those alternatives lack.
3. **Value.** What those attributes let customers achieve, ideally with evidence.
4. **Target customers.** The people who care most about that value.
5. **Market category.** The frame you place the product in, which tells customers what to compare it with and what to expect.

**Differentiation is relative to alternatives.** A feature differentiates only if the alternative lacks it and customers care what it enables. "Has search" is not a difference if the spreadsheet already has search.

**Start narrow.** Choose the segment for whom your value is strongest. They are easier to reach, the message can be specific, and they tell each other. Wider segments come after you win there.

## Worked example

A team builds an app for splitting and tracking group-project work. Their first pitch, "a project management app for everyone", invites comparison with established tools such as Trello, Notion and Asana, and it loses on almost every feature.

Working through the components:

- **Alternatives:** for university students, a group chat plus a shared document.
- **Unique attributes:** tasks tied to assignment deadlines, and a log of who did what.
- **Value:** nobody's work goes unnoticed at peer-evaluation time, and nobody has to chase teammates in the chat.
- **Target:** students in project-heavy modules with peer evaluation.
- **Category:** "group assignment tracker", not "project management software".

Now the comparison is with the chat and the document, where the app clearly wins.

## Failure modes

- **Comparing to the wrong alternative.** Benchmarking against the market leader when customers actually use a spreadsheet or nothing.
- **Feature list as positioning.** Attributes with no value or alternative attached. Customers respond "so what?"
- **Targeting everyone.** The message is vague and no group feels it is for them.
- **Wrong category.** A frame that puts you against stronger products, or one customers do not recognise.
- **Positioning never revisited.** The founding idea stays the story after users show the product's real strength lies elsewhere.

## Checks

```check
q: A team says its note app is differentiated because it has tags and full-text search. What should they check before claiming that?
a: What their target customers use instead, and whether that alternative already has tags and search. A feature differentiates only if the alternative lacks it and customers value what it enables.
why: Catches judging features in isolation instead of against alternatives.
```

```check
q: For a new invoicing tool aimed at freelance designers, why might a spreadsheet be a more important competitive alternative than the leading invoicing software?
a: Because many of those designers invoice from a spreadsheet or by hand today. Positioning has to beat what customers actually do now, not the product the team thinks of as its rival.
why: Catches equating competitive alternatives with named rival products.
```

```check
q: The same app can be framed as "project management software" or as a "group assignment tracker". What changes when you pick the category?
a: The comparison customers make and what they expect. The broad category sets it against mature tools where it loses; the narrow one sets it against a chat and a shared document, where it wins.
why: Catches treating the market category as a label rather than the frame for comparison.
```
